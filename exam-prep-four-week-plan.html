How to prepare for exams in four weeks

If your exams are four weeks away, you have enough time to cover the syllabus once and revise it properly. This guide gives you the order of work, a daily load you can keep, and a way to check that you are on track.

Last updated 14 November 2026

Start with the syllabus, not the textbook

Write out every topic in the course outline. Next to each one, mark it as known, partly known or not seen. This list is your map for the month. If you do not have the outline, ask your course representative or lecturer which topics the exam covers.

Most students spend too long on topics they already understand. The map stops that. Your first job is to find the gaps.

Week 1: learn what you have not seen

Give each topic in the not seen list about one hour. Read your lecture notes first, then the matching section of the textbook. At the end, write a one-page summary in your own words.

Do not try to memorise everything this week. The goal is to understand the shape of each topic.

Week 2: cover the rest and start testing yourself

Finish any topics that are still not clear. After each topic, answer two or three questions from memory without looking at your notes. Keep a list of the questions you got wrong, because that list will guide the rest of your revision.

Week 3: past questions and weak topics

Start doing past questions under timed conditions. Spend about 60 percent of your study time on the topics that appear in your wrong-answer list. Review the answers afterwards and note the method you should have used.

Week 4: review and rest

Use your one-page summaries and the wrong-answer list for quick review. In the last two days, do not start new topics. Keep your sleep time steady so that you are alert in the exam room.

A daily load you can keep

Three study blocks of 50 minutes, with a 10-minute break between them, is a sensible day for most students. That is about four hours of focused work. More than that usually means you are tired and your notes are not sinking in.

Keep at least half of one day free each week. A rested mind remembers more than a tired one.

How to tell if you are on track

  • By the end of week 1, every topic has been studied at least once.
  • By the end of week 2, you have answered questions on every topic.
  • By the end of week 3, your list of wrong answers is getting shorter.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with the subject you like best, and leaving the hardest one for the last week.
  • Re-reading notes without testing yourself. Reading feels productive, but it is a weak way to remember.
  • Studying until midnight the night before the exam. Sleep helps memory, so protect it.

Frequently asked questions

What if I have fewer than four weeks?

Shorten weeks 1 and 2 into one week. Focus only on the topics marked not seen, and then spend the rest of the time on past questions.

Should I study with a group?

Yes, but for explaining rather than for reading together. Explain a topic to someone else without notes. If you cannot, you have found a gap. Keep group sessions short and focused.
